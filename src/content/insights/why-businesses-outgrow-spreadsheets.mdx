Spreadsheets are the most successful business software ever made. They are flexible, familiar and available on every computer. Most businesses I talk to started their operations in one — and that was the right decision.

The problem is that spreadsheets fail gradually. Nothing breaks on a particular day. Things just get a little slower, a little less certain, until the business is working around its own tools.

## The signs

**Nobody is sure which file is correct.** There is `stock.xlsx`, `stock-final.xlsx` and a version someone emailed on Tuesday.

**The same data is typed twice.** An order is entered in one sheet for sales and again in another for production.

**Only one person understands the formulas.** When they are on leave, the reports stop.

**Permissions are all or nothing.** Everyone who can see the sheet can also change it, or delete it.

**Reporting is a project.** A monthly summary takes a day of copying, filtering and checking.

## What a system changes

The value of business software is rarely a feature. It is **one source of truth**: every order, customer and stock movement recorded once, in one place, with a history of who changed what.

From there, the rest becomes possible — role-based access, automatic reports, notifications when something needs attention, and integrations with the website or accounting tools.

## Don't replace everything at once

The worst way to leave spreadsheets is a big-bang migration. A better approach:

1. Pick the workflow that causes the most pain.
2. Build a small system for that workflow and import the existing data.
3. Run it alongside the spreadsheet briefly, then retire the sheet.
4. Move to the next workflow.

Each step delivers value on its own, and the team learns the new system gradually instead of all at once.

## Sometimes the answer is to buy

Custom software is not always the answer. If an existing product fits how you work, use it. Custom systems earn their cost when your process is part of what makes you different, or when off-the-shelf tools force expensive workarounds.

The question to ask is simple: *are we changing how we work to fit the tool?* If the answer is yes and it hurts, it may be time to build.
